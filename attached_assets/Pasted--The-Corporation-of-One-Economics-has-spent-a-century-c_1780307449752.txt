# The Corporation of One

Economics has spent a century congratulating itself on refuting Adam Smith, and most of the refutation is a misreading. The behavioral literature produces its counterexamples in bulk: people are loss-averse, they honor sunk costs, they don't optimize, they choose against their own interest. Each finding is offered as another nail in the coffin of the rational actor. But the rational actor was never a claim about people. Smith's earner is defined entirely by its interface with the market — a unit that buys inputs, sells outputs, faces the price signal directly, and adjusts its conduct to it. That is a description of a *structural role*, not a species trait. It fits a self-employed butcher and it fits General Motors, because both are market-facing units. What it does not fit is a person *qua* person, and Smith never claimed it did. The "individual" in the model was always a corporation of one — the limiting case where the firm and the human being happen to occupy the same body.

This is why the wage-earner looks like a counterexample and isn't. The employee does not transact with the market; he transacts with the firm, and the firm transacts with the market. He is not an interface to the price system but a component inside a unit that has one. So when he behaves "irrationally" — ignoring opportunity cost, failing to optimize, treating his salary as weather rather than as the output of specific acts — he is not refuting the model. He is demonstrating that *persons qua persons* do not behave like market-facing units, which was never in dispute. The optimizing logic runs through the entity that stands in the market, and the employee, structurally, is not that entity. He is closer to an organ than an agent. The behavioral economist who points at him and cries "counterexample" has confused a cell with the organism.

The honest correction is not to abandon Smith but to rename his unit. Stop calling it "the individual," which smuggles in a psychological subject, and call it what it structurally is: the earner. The earner is whatever entity directly engages the market and bears the consequences of getting the price wrong. In Smith's day, when most production was conducted by self-employed people and household enterprises, the earner and the person largely coincided, which is why the model read so naturally as a theory of human beings. In a wage economy, they come apart — the rational actor migrates up a level into the firm, which behaves with exactly the price-responsiveness Smith predicted, while the human inside it is along for the ride. Smith's model didn't fail. It just stopped describing most people, because most people stopped being earners.
