What They Think They Are Doing
The preceding articles examined two bodies of work: Falvey's account of self-knowledge and externalism, and Edgington's treatment of conditionals, vagueness, and the preface paradox. The verdict in each case was the same. Falvey defends authority over the avowal while the real loss — knowledge of which content one is thinking — goes untouched, and protects content externalism against the obvious fact that one knows what one is thinking about. Edgington identifies a statement with a number, setting "if A then B" equal to the conditional probability P(B|A), and flattens the heterogeneous commitments of a real author into a uniform scale of credences so that an arithmetic he supplies will appear necessary. A single procedure runs through both: take an entrenched doctrine as a fixed datum, and when it collides with something plainer and more certain than itself, sacrifice the plain fact and build apparatus to make the sacrifice look principled. The question that remains is what Falvey and Edgington take themselves to be doing while they do this.
The obvious answers fail at once. They are not pursuing truth; the procedure consists in shielding a doctrine from the facts that refute it, which is the opposite of caring whether it is true. They are not solving problems; the problems are artifacts they manufacture by flattening some plain reality into a form a machine can process, so that the machine becomes necessary — remove the flattening and the problem vanishes, which means there was never a problem, only a pretext. And they are not advancing understanding, since understanding is what the apparatus destroys: sound, ordinary comprehension is paved over precisely to clear ground for the construction. The first tier of answers is thus not merely unsupported but inverted. Each names what the activity works against.
The less obvious answers fail harder, because they are the ones Falvey and Edgington would themselves offer. "I am being rigorous" is false: rigor is answerability to the subject matter, and the one load-bearing premise — that a statement can be identical to a number, that introspection is a quasi-perception that might silently fail — is exactly what is never examined, left alone not by oversight but because examining it ends everything. Great care is spent on every inference downstream of the error and none on the error itself; that is not rigor but its costume. "They are skillful dialecticians" is a complete and total lie. Equating a statement with a number is not a deft maneuver but a crude blunder a careful amateur would catch; what passes for skill is mere fluency in the house style — the ability to produce sentences in the cadence of serious philosophy. That is mimicry, and mimicry is not competence. What is left, once every dignified answer is stripped away, is small and exact: they believe they are succeeding — winning the only game they track, in which work the profession receives as serious is rewarded with the chair, the citation, the festschrift. And they are wrong even about that, for they read the reward as proof of ability when it proves only that the system grades conformity to a register rather than contact with the world. The apparatus that pays them cannot distinguish the fluent-and-empty from the genuine, and certifies the first as readily as the second. Falvey and Edgington mistook their standing in that apparatus for skill, and their fluency for thought. What they think they are doing is excelling. What they are in fact doing is performing the sound of excellence for the one audience too compromised to hear that nothing has been said.
