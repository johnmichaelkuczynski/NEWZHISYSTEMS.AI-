# The Needle Becomes the Balloon: Why Old Comedians Stop Being Funny

There is a puzzle in the way great old writers and great old comedians age in opposite directions. Tolstoy and Freud got *better* with age, right up until their bodies quit; their material was the permanent weight of being alive—death, desire, self-deception—and time only deepened their access to it. But Chevy Chase is not funny anymore, and Bill Murray is funny mostly as a memory. This is not senility. It is something stranger, and it becomes clear only once you accept the right theory of what a laugh actually is. Comedy is not fundamentally about surprise or cleverness. Following Freud, it is about *release*: a joke smuggles some forbidden or suppressed pressure past our internal censor, and the laugh is the discharge of the energy we had been spending to hold that pressure down. No pressure, no refund, no laugh. You cannot vent a tank that is already empty—which is why a political cartoon about Taft, fully legible, is completely dead.

Once you see comedy as release, the fate of the aging comedian stops being a mystery and becomes a matter of structure. The comedian's raw material is the reigning *weight*—the pious, high, over-serious thing the room is quietly straining under—and his function is to be the needle that punctures it. But that role has a hidden requirement: he must be *low* relative to the thing he deflates. Chevy Chase pratfalling as Gerald Ford worked because Ford was the weight—the office, the gravity, the weekly obligation to revere power—and Chase was the nimble nobody letting the air out. The energy released was the audience's own suppressed irreverence, and Chase was merely the instrument it escaped through. His funniness was never really his. It was borrowed from the altitude of whatever he was puncturing.

And that is exactly why success destroys him. Success promotes the puncturer into the very position he used to puncture. Chase became an institution—a legend, a thing you are *instructed* to find brilliant—which means he stopped being the needle and became the balloon. He is now the weight: the reverence, the pressure, the pious thing the room labors under, where "oh, he's *so* funny" is the burden Gerald Ford once was to him. The energy that used to flow *through* him now pools *against* him, and no one can be their own valve. He did not lose the skill; the skill became structurally impossible to deploy, because you cannot simultaneously be the reverence and the puncturing of it. This is why the aging novelist ripens and the aging comedian curdles. The novelist's material was always external and permanent. The comedian's material was his own low position—and success is precisely the process of losing it. He is killed by the thing he spent his life chasing, and the applause is the tell that the funny is already dead.
