# Why the Childish Story Wins: Legibility, Not Truth

The Hobbesian picture of human and international life — order is force, power is a ladder, the strong take and the weak are taken — survives not because it is true but because it is *legible*. "My dad can beat up your dad" is a single number on a single axis. More force defeats less force; rank everyone accordingly; the model is complete. It fits in working memory, it produces unambiguous orderings, it can be taught in one sentence and drawn on a whiteboard with one arrow. That is its entire competitive advantage, and in the marketplace of ideas — especially the academic and policy marketplace — compressibility beats correspondence almost every time. The story is not winning the argument. It is winning the *compression contest*, and we keep mistaking the prize for evidence.

The actual story has no scalar to win that contest with. What stabilizes a human group is a high-dimensional tangle: kinship bonds shot through with sublimated aggression, real aggression partly displaced outward onto external foes, and an enormous amount of it *metabolized* through ritual — high-school sports, litigation, status games, the thousand safe channels by which the species processes hostility instead of discharging it. It does not reduce to a number or a direction. It is emphatically not "my team beats your team," and it is just as emphatically not the sentimental inversion — "we all get along, we need no borders." It is the thing *between* those two slogans, the thing neither slogan can hold. And a truth that no slogan can hold is, in a world that selects for the publishable and the teachable, functionally treated as no truth at all. The realist did not defeat this picture on the evidence. He defeated it on formalizability, then mistook his own tractability for rigor.

This is the specific pathology of the academy, which selects for legibility even harder than the culture at large, because its currency *is* the model — the indexable variable, the regressable quantity, the artifact you can build a syllabus around. "Balance of power" hands you a number; you can chart it, correlate it, defend a dissertation on it. The correct account — that nations and communities are held together by delicate, illegible webs of kinship, trust, shared value, property guarantee, and access to the flowing currents of productivity — hands you nothing to put on the x-axis. It is true and unmodelable, which in that environment is indistinguishable from false. So the childish story doesn't merely persist; it gets promoted to *default scenario*, the baseline against which every subtler account must justify itself, purely because it is the one picture cheap enough to represent. The burden of proof gets assigned, perversely, to reality.

And here is the sharpest irony, the one that should have killed the realist's pretension to hard-headedness long ago: the illegible, correct account often reaches the very conclusions the realist thinks he owns — but for inverted reasons that expose how little he understood his own position. Yes, you need borders. Yes, you need enforcement and hard property guarantees. But not because Nigeria might storm in and loot Iceland's crops — *nobody cares* whether Nigeria could militarily decimate Iceland, because in a world where wealth is tapped rather than seized there are no crops to loot, no asset that survives the looting. You need borders to *preserve the delicate, high-dimensional thing the legible story cannot even see*: the kinship relations, the trust networks, the property regime, the standing access to the wealth-currents that make a place rich in the first place. The realist is right about the fence and wrong about everything on both sides of it. He is reading the one variable his model can fit and calling the rest of reality noise — which is not tough-mindedness. It is the opposite: the soft option of refusing to look at anything you can't put a number on, dressed up as courage.
