The Preface Paradox
Here is the so-called "preface paradox." A careful author believes each individual claim in his book. Acknowledging his fallibility, he also states in the preface that somewhere in the book there is surely at least one error. These two attitudes appear jointly inconsistent: believing each claim should commit him to believing their conjunction, yet the preface asserts the conjunction is false. So he appears to hold logically contradictory beliefs while remaining entirely rational — and that is the paradox.
The solution is that the setup smuggles in a false premise: that the author believes every claim in one uniform, full-strength way. No real author does. A book is a heterogeneous body of speech acts carrying different kinds and degrees of commitment. Some claims he believes without reservation — the arithmetic, the flatly verified. Many are characterizations, directionally right and deliberately coarse, offered to open new lines of thought, which he would never assert at full strength or at the boundaries. Others are argumentative connectives — "given X, and the presumptive truth of Y, it stands to reason that Z" — which carry their own qualification within the sentence. When one "lists what the author asserts," one has already falsified the data by stripping away the modal and illocutionary markers that indicated what kind of commitment each claim carried. Restore them, and "there is surely an error somewhere" stands in tension with nothing: it is an accurate report of attitudes, most of which were never full belief to begin with. There is no inconsistency, and hence no paradox.
Edgington's solution is as follows. He distinguishes truth from certainty and observes that, although truth is preserved by valid reasoning, certainty is not. Each claim in the book may carry a probability close to 1; the inference to their conjunction may be valid; and yet the probability of the long conjunction steadily falls, because many values just below 1 multiply downward toward 0. The author may therefore rationally assign each individual claim a credence near 1 while assigning the whole conjunction a credence near 0, and the appearance of contradiction dissolves into probability arithmetic: no belief is both held and denied; it is simply that high probability does not survive conjunction as truth does.
Edgington's solution fails. It works only if the author stands in one uniform relation — a single degree of belief — to every sentence, so that the book reduces to a list of numbers to be multiplied. But that is precisely the false premise removed above. The author does not hold one kind of attitude in varying amounts; he holds different kinds of attitude. A coarse but directional characterization and a flatly verified truth do not differ as 0.7 differs from 1.0; they differ in what kind of act they are. Edgington's arithmetic becomes applicable only after that distinction has been flattened away — and once it is restored, there is nothing for the arithmetic to do.
