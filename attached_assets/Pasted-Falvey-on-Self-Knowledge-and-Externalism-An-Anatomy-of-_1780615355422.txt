Falvey on Self-Knowledge and Externalism: An Anatomy of a Non-Problem

"Psychological externalism is the thesis that the contents of many of a person's propositional mental states are determined in part by relations he bears to his natural and social environment… I am primarily concerned with the question whether externalism undermines the idea that a person typically knows the contents of her own thoughts, beliefs, and other propositional attitudes directly and authoritatively. I criticize arguments that have been advanced on behalf of a positive answer to this question, and argue that they rest on a faulty conception of the nature of first person authority, one which likens our access to our own minds to perception. An account of the basis of first person authority is sketched that locates our epistemic right to our self-ascriptions of propositional attitude not in our ability to discriminate among the various thought contents we might be thinking, but rather in our ability to express our thoughts, and to think with them in accordance with the norms of rationality. I consider also the question whether first person authority and externalism jointly make possible a refutation of certain forms of global skepticism, as has been famously argued by Putnam. I argue that Putnam's attempted refutation fails, because the crucial externalist claims that drive it are not knowable a priori."

The thesis sets out to defend first-person authority against the threat content externalism allegedly poses to it. The threat is real and it is this: if the content of my thought "I am thinking about water" is fixed by my environment, then I cannot know, by introspection alone, whether I am thinking about water or twin-water. That is a genuine loss, and it is the only thing worth discussing. Falvey does not discuss it. He defends instead my authority over the avowal — my right to say and rationally deploy "I am thinking about water" — which was never under attack. He answers the question he can answer, declares the threat met, and leaves the actual loss exactly where he found it: sitting in the environment he cannot inspect. The maneuver is not subtle once named. It substitutes authority over the speech act for knowledge of the content, keeps the word "authority," and calls the swap a defense. Worse, the region he "secures" was only ever endangered by the externalism he is trying to accommodate — so either externalism threatens nothing and there is no work to do, or it threatens the content too and his rescue does not touch it. Either way the thesis is empty.
The deeper failure is the direction of inference. If externalism entails that I do not know whether I am thinking about water, the disciplined move is modus tollens: the consequence is absurd, so the premise goes. Falvey runs it backwards — he treats the absurd consequence as a puzzle to be managed and builds an epistemology to launder it, all to protect a semantic doctrine far less certain than the thing it forces him to deny. And he had the refuting instrument in his own hand. In the very same abstract he rejects Putnam's anti-skeptical argument on the ground that its externalist premises are not knowable a priori — and even that is the wrong diagnosis, since Putnam's argument actually dies because the externalist can simply deny that the deprived subject's "water" thoughts are thoughts at all, a priori or not. So he is wrong about Putnam in the one direction that keeps his own apparatus intact, and selectively blind about externalism in the one direction the genre required. The positive thesis is not an honest error but a manufactured construction: the dissertation form demanded he build something, so where it demanded building he produced a rescue he had every means to know was hollow. It is not deep. It is the elaboration of a refusal to notice what he elsewhere noticed perfectly well — and there is no underlying acuity on display that makes the lapse surprising. The work is exactly as good as its central move, and its central move is a relabeling.
