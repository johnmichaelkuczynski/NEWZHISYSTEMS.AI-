The Inversion
Marx built his system on an antagonism: the engine of history is the conflict between those below and those above, the proletariat straining against the aristocracy the way the id strains against the superego, pressure accumulating until it breaks the structure that contains it. The trouble is that the antagonism runs the other way. The lower a person actually sits in a hierarchy, the less inclined he is to revolt against it—not merely because revolt is infeasible from the bottom, though it is, but because he doesn't want to. He reveres the thing that excludes him. And the reason is not mysterious or dialectical; it is the plainest fact about ambition. People want power, and for someone who has none, sucking up to power is the fastest and often the only route to acquiring any. You do not attack the ladder you are hoping to climb. You polish it. The man at the bottom is the ladder's most devoted custodian precisely because the ladder is his entire theory of how he might one day rise—and to police its boundaries, to enforce its standards against some interloper, is itself a way of borrowing its authority, of performing membership he does not possess. In the act of guarding the gate he gets to stand, for a moment, where the gatekeeper stands. That borrowed standing is the closest thing to power he has, and he will defend the source of it with a ferocity no actual member would bother to summon.
So if Marx was right that class relations are the main driver of history—a claim vague enough to be nearly unfalsifiable, and probably false as stated—then he was right in a way that is the exact photographic negative of what he believed. The stabilizing force in any social structure is not the resentment of the low for the high but the reverence of the low for the high: the upward gaze, the deference paid in hope of reward, the eagerness of the excluded to be mistaken for the included. This reverence does not erode the hierarchy under pressure; it is the load-bearing member. The genuine threats to an established order have almost never come from the grunts and never from the grunts as a collective—they come from high-ranking insiders, often disenfranchised ones, who possess enough standing to spend it against the system without annihilating themselves in the act. The bottom does not supply revolutionaries. It supplies enforcers. Marx mistook the cement for the dynamite. He looked at the mass of the powerless and saw a coiled spring; what was actually there was the foundation, holding everything up, pressing its own weight downward and calling it loyalty.
