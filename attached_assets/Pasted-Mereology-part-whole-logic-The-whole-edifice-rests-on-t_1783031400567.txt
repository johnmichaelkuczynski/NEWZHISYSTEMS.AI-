Mereology (part-whole logic). The whole edifice rests on the transitivity axiom: if X is part of Y and Y is part of Z, then X is part of Z. This is false, and trivially so once you look at levels of organization. An atom composes your liver; your liver is part of you; but the atom is not a part of you in the way the liver is. The liver is a functional constituent of an organism; the atom is a material constituent of tissue. "Part of" means something different at each level — material, functional, personal — and transitivity flattens all of that into a single uniform relation that doesn't exist. When pressed, the mereologist either multiplies "kinds" of parthood (thereby conceding the axiom was false as stated) or bites the bullet and calls the atom a part of you (which is absurd). Either way the axiom fails, and once it fails there's nothing left. Mereology doesn't discover the structure of parthood; it stipulates a tidy structure and ignores every case that breaks it.
Epistemic logic. The load-bearing axiom is the KK principle: K(p) → K(K(p)) — if you know p, you know that you know p. Flatly false. A cat knows Fido is charging and acts on it, but the cat has no concept of "knowledge" or "self," so it cannot know that it knows. The standard escape — deny that cats have propositional beliefs — doesn't save the principle, because KK is false even for full-blooded believers: a farmer can know corn prices will fall without knowing that he knows it, and possibly without being able to arrive at that second-order knowledge even under instruction. The only way to rescue the axiom is to define "knowledge" so narrowly that only an actively introspecting philosopher qualifies — which is circular, ad hoc, and makes the "principle" true by fiat rather than by insight. It's clever formalism built on a foundation that a cat refutes.
Deontic logic. Here the sacred axiom is "ought implies can": O(p) → C(p). Lose a bet, owe a man a million dollars — you are obligated to pay, therefore, per the axiom, you can. Except you can't, which is why collections agencies, foreclosures, and the entire history of credit and banking exist. The whole lending industry runs on the opposite principle: people are routinely obligated to pay what they cannot pay. The dodge is to redefine "can" as bare logical or physical possibility ("you could rob a bank, sell your house, get a second job"), but that empties the axiom of content — it's now vacuously true and says nothing about actual obligation. Real obligation tracks actual capacity, not metaphysical possibility, and deontic logic gets this exactly backwards while congratulating itself on rigor.
Temporal logic. The axioms are either trivial or question-begging. The distribution axiom, G(φ→ψ)→(Gφ→Gψ), is just modus ponens with "always" bolted on — true by definition, teaching nothing. The interesting-looking axioms (linearity, no circular timelines, infinite past and future) don't discover these features of time; they stipulate them, banning branching futures and temporal boundaries by assumption. And on the questions that actually matter it is silent. Is entropy-increase a contingent or necessary feature of the passage of time? Is signal-transmission written into the very concept of temporal precedence, or merely a contingent fact of our universe (yes in Einstein, no in Newton — but only because Newton was wrong)? Temporal logic has nothing to say, because it models ordering, not time. It knows no physics, no causation, no entropy. It is a formal skeleton mistaken for a theory, and its practitioners use the apparatus precisely to avoid the questions the apparatus can't touch.
Possible-world semantics. The framework claims to explain modality by quantifying over possible worlds — necessary means true in all of them, contingent means true in some. But ask what a possible world is and the whole thing collapses into a dilemma. If a "world" is just a maximal set of consistent statements, then you're not doing modal metaphysics at all — you're doing syntax and consistency, and the modal force has to come from somewhere else you haven't explained. If a "world" is a genuine concrete cosmos, then the machinery does no explanatory work, because evaluating a proposition "across worlds" presupposes that its key terms keep a fixed meaning from world to world. And that presupposition fails exactly where the metaphysics gets interesting: if "time" in a Newtonian world and "time" in an Einsteinian world aren't the same thing, then asking whether the Lorentzian structure of time is "necessary" or "contingent" isn't a modal question the framework can answer — it's a pseudo-question the framework is built to conceal. Possible worlds don't dissolve the hard cases; they hide them behind notation.
