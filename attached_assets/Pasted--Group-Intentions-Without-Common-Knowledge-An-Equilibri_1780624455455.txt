# Group Intentions Without Common Knowledge: An Equilibrium of Interlocking Roles

## Abstract

A familiar program in social ontology analyzes group intentions deflationarily — as a configuration of the mental states of individual members rather than as a sui generis collective mind — and then characterizes that configuration as a kind of *equilibrium*. Both moves are correct. The trouble lies in the standard build-out, which specifies the equilibrium in epistemic terms: a group intends that *p* only when there is iterated mutual knowledge (or common knowledge, or common belief) of the relevant attitudes among its members. This paper argues that the epistemic build-out is not merely unnecessary but a misdescription of the phenomenon. Collective intention is most unmistakable precisely where iterated mutual knowledge is most absent — in ant colonies, in large firms, and in armies, whose members are massively opaque to one another. What each member actually possesses is not a tower of beliefs about other members' beliefs but *role-knowledge* together with *role-trust*: the acceptance of oneself as a functional part of a team, governed by rules one expects to be reciprocally honored. The equilibrium that constitutes a group intention is therefore an equilibrium over interlocking role-commitments — a stability in the meshing of functions — not a convergence of higher-order beliefs. I show how this reading preserves everything the deflationary and equilibrium insights were after while jettisoning a cognitive precondition that the clearest cases of collective intention flatly lack.

## 1. Two Right Ideas and One Wrong One

Start with two claims that ought to be uncontroversial.

The first is deflationary. When a group intends something, we need not posit a further entity — a group mind, a collective consciousness, a plural subject hovering above its members — to bear the intention. Whatever a group intention is, it is constituted by the states and activities of the individuals who make up the group. To say that a committee intends to publish its report by Friday is not to attribute a mental act to some super-agent; it is to say something about the members and how they are arranged. This much is correct, and I will simply grant it.

The second claim is that the relevant arrangement is an *equilibrium*. A group intention is not a heap of individual intentions but a configuration that is, in some sense, self-sustaining: given how the members are disposed, none has reason to defect from the part he is playing, and the parts reinforce one another. This too is correct. Coordination that deserves the name "intention" must be stable under the members' own responses to it; a configuration that unravels the moment each member consults his own interest was never a group intention in the first place. The equilibrium idea captures this stability, and it does so without inflation, since an equilibrium is just a property of how individual states stand to one another.

The wrong idea is the standard way of cashing out the equilibrium. On the orthodox picture — descending from Lewis on convention, Aumann on interactive epistemology, and Bratman on shared intention — the equilibrium is *epistemic*. The members not only intend their respective parts; they know that the others so intend, and know that the others know this, and so on, ideally without limit. The group intention is supposed to live in this lattice of mutual knowledge. My thesis is that this is a category mistake about what the equilibrium ranges over. The equilibrium ranges over *roles and their meshing*, not over *beliefs and their iteration*. Where these come apart — and they come apart constantly — collective intention tracks the former and is indifferent to the latter.

## 2. The Common-Knowledge Orthodoxy

The orthodox apparatus is worth stating precisely, because its appeal is real and its defect is hidden in the machinery rather than on its surface.

Lewis analyzed a convention as a regularity in behavior sustained because it is common knowledge in the population that everyone prefers to conform on condition that others do. The driving thought is that mere matching behavior is not yet a convention; what makes it a convention is that each agent conforms *because* he expects others to, and expects them to expect him to, and so on. The higher-order expectations are doing constitutive work. Aumann gave the epistemic side of this picture its canonical formal treatment. Agents are modeled by information partitions over a state space; an event is common knowledge at a state when it holds throughout the cell of the *meet* of the agents' partitions containing that state — that is, when it survives the coarsest common refinement of what each agent can distinguish. From this it follows, famously, that agents with a common prior cannot "agree to disagree": if their posteriors for an event are common knowledge, the posteriors are equal. Common knowledge, on this construction, is an extremely demanding condition — it requires that the relevant fact be robust across an entire iterated structure of who-can-distinguish-what.

Bratman carried the apparatus into the theory of intention proper. On his account, we intend to do something jointly when I intend that we do it and you intend that we do it, each intends that we do it by way of the other's intention and by way of meshing subplans, and all of this is common knowledge between us. The common-knowledge condition is not incidental decoration; it is what is supposed to convert two parallel individual intentions into a genuinely *shared* one. Searle resisted the reductive program with his irreducible "we-intentions," and Gilbert resisted it with "joint commitment" and the "plural subject," but even the resisters tend to retain something in the neighborhood of mutual awareness: Gilbert's joint commitment is formed when parties openly express, in conditions of common knowledge, their readiness to be jointly committed. Across the field, then, however the parties divide on reduction, the iterated-knowledge condition is treated as close to a fixed point.

It is this fixed point I mean to dislodge. The apparatus is elegant and, for certain narrow human coordination problems — two strangers trying to reconvene in a city, a pair settling on which side of the road to drive — it is even apt. The error is to generalize it into the *constitution* of group intention as such. What works as a model of how two transparent agents might bootstrap coordination from scratch has been mistaken for an analysis of what coordination *is*.

## 3. Three Cases

The quickest way to see the defect is to look at the cases where group intention is least disputable and ask whether the iterated-knowledge condition obtains there. It does not.

**Ant colonies.** A colony unmistakably has collective intentions. It forages, defends the nest, relocates the brood, wages war on neighboring colonies, farms fungus, herds aphids. These are not metaphors imposed by a charitable observer; they are coordinated, goal-directed activities of the colony as a unit, robust across the loss of individual ants and flexible in the face of obstacles — exactly the marks we use to attribute intention anywhere. Yet there is no ant that knows what another ant intends, much less knows that another ant knows what a third intends. There is no theory of mind anywhere in the system. The coordination is *stigmergic*: each ant responds to local cues — pheromone gradients, contact rates, simple thresholds — and the global intention emerges from the interlocking of these local responses. Here collective intention is at its most glaring and iterated mutual knowledge is at its most completely absent. If a condition can be wholly missing exactly where the phenomenon is most evident, the condition is not constitutive of the phenomenon. The lower organisms are not a marginal case to be quarantined; they are the cleanest demonstration that group intention does not require minds reading minds.

One might protest that ants merely *behave as if* they intend, lacking the real article. But the deflationary thesis we granted in §1 forecloses this move for the human case too. If human group intention is nothing over and above the arrangement of members' states and activities, then the relevant question for the colony is exactly the same: is there a self-sustaining arrangement of the parts' activities directed at a goal? Plainly there is. One cannot help oneself to deflationism about human groups and then suddenly demand an inner glow from ants to certify the colony's intention as genuine.

**Firms.** A corporation intends to acquire a competitor, to discontinue a product line, to settle a lawsuit. The intention is real and consequential — it shapes contracts, employment, markets. But there is no proposition about the acquisition that is common knowledge across the firm. The clerk in the mailroom does not know what the board knows; the board does not know what the line engineer knows; legal, finance, and operations each hold non-overlapping fragments, and large parts of the relevant information are deliberately compartmentalized. The org chart is a structure of *informational opacity*, not transparency. If group intention required the iterated mutual knowledge the orthodoxy specifies, no firm of any size could ever intend anything, since at scale the meet of the members' information partitions is nearly trivial — almost nothing survives as common knowledge across the whole organization. Yet firms intend constantly and effectively. What coordinates them is not shared belief but shared *structure*: a division of labor in which each part is locally faithful to a defined function, and the functions are arranged so that their joint operation realizes the corporate aim.

**Armies.** The army case sharpens the point to a single edge. An army has a group intention if anything does — to take the ridge, to hold the line, to encircle the enemy. But consider the epistemic situation of its members. The private does not know the plan; he knows his orders and his sector. The captain has a more local picture, but he does not know the operational design above him. Even the general barely knows "what is happening" in any rich sense — the fog of war guarantees that no one commands a synoptic view, which is the whole reason armies are built as they are. What every member *does* know is something far thinner and far more robust: *I am on this team; there are rules I am not to break and that I expect others not to break; and those rules define my function within the team.* That is the entire cognitive content required of him, and it is not a belief about anyone else's beliefs. It is knowledge of his own role plus an expectation that others will keep theirs. The general's ignorance and the private's ignorance do not impede the army's intention in the slightest, because the intention was never located in their mutual transparency. It was located in the meshing of their roles.

## 4. Mechanism Versus Phenomenon

The three cases license a single diagnosis. The orthodoxy has confused a *mechanism* for achieving coordination with the *phenomenon* of coordination itself.

Iterated mutual knowledge is one mechanism by which coordination can be reached — a parochial, expensive, and distinctively (though not exclusively) human one. When two agents have no antecedent structure of roles to fall back on, they can sometimes bootstrap coordination by reasoning about each other: I'll go where I expect you to expect me to go. This is real, and the Lewis–Aumann apparatus models it well. But it is a special and demanding route to coordination, available only to agents with sophisticated theory of mind, adequate common information, and the cognitive budget to run the recursion. It is no surprise that the philosophers who built the apparatus had such agents in mind, since they typically illustrated their theories with pairs of well-informed adults solving toy problems.

The phenomenon of group intention is vastly more general than this mechanism. It is realized in colonies, hives, firms, armies, orchestras, and emergency-room teams, most of which cannot and do not run the recursion. What is common to all of them is not converging higher-order belief but a *self-sustaining interlock of functional parts*. The recursion, where it occurs at all, is a sometimes-helpful add-on for a narrow class of agents in a narrow class of situations — never the thing itself. To define group intention by the recursion is like defining flight by the flapping of feathered wings: one salient mechanism mistaken for the function it sometimes serves.

There is a further, psychological count against the recursion even in the human case. No one actually entertains more than two or three levels of "I know that you know that I know." Beyond that shallow depth the iteration becomes a verbal formula with no answering mental act — a notation, not a thought. So even where the orthodoxy seems most at home, its official condition is not psychologically real. The members are not running an infinite lattice of beliefs; they are doing something cheaper, which the next section identifies.

## 5. Role-Commitment Equilibrium

Here is the positive account. A group intends that *p* when its members stand in a *role-commitment equilibrium* with respect to *p*: each member accepts a functional role, the roles are arranged so that their joint discharge realizes *p*, each is disposed to discharge his role on the standing expectation that others will discharge theirs, and this arrangement is stable — no member has reason to defect from his role given that the roles mesh and the governing rules hold.

Three features distinguish this from the orthodox account.

First, the basic attitude is not belief but *acceptance of a role* — a stance, a taking-up of a function, closer to commitment than to representation. When the private knows he is on the team, he is not primarily representing facts about his comrades' minds; he has *taken a place* in a structure and undertaken to hold it. This is why the account is normative through and through: a role comes with rules, with things one is "not to break," and with a corresponding entitlement to expect reciprocity from the holders of adjacent roles. Gilbert was right that joint commitment is normative and not merely descriptive; she was wrong to think the commitment had to be established through openly expressed, commonly known declarations. The shepherd dog and the sheepdog-handler coordinate; the worker ant and the soldier ant coordinate; new employees inherit functioning roles they never negotiated and whose occupants they never meet. The normativity is carried by the role-architecture, not minted afresh by mutual declaration.

Second, what each member must know is *local* and *bounded*: his own function and the rules that constrain it, plus a general trust that the surrounding roles will be honored. He need not know the other roles in detail, need not know who occupies them, and certainly need not know what their occupants know. Role-knowledge replaces mutual knowledge. This is exactly why the structure scales — to the size of a corporation, an army, a colony — where mutual knowledge cannot. Opacity between parts is not a defect the structure tolerates; it is a condition the structure is *designed to exploit*, freeing each part from the impossible burden of comprehending the whole.

Third, the trust each member places in the others is *role-trust*, not inferred belief about their psychology. The private expects his flank to hold not because he has modeled the soldier on his flank but because that is the soldier's *job* and the soldier is, presumably, on the team. Role-trust is a default attached to the role, defeasible by evidence of breakdown, and it is what does the work the orthodoxy assigned to higher-order belief. It is cheap, it is robust, and it is psychologically real in a way the infinite lattice never was.

## 6. What "Equilibrium" Means Here

The equilibrium concept survives the reform; it is simply relocated from epistemic states to roles and actions.

Recall that the orthodoxy located the equilibrium in the *meet of information partitions* — the coarsest structure of mutual distinguishability, which is precisely what collapses to triviality in any large or opaque group. Replace this with a fixed point in the space of role-behaviors. The members' role-discharges are in equilibrium when each is a stable response to the others: given that the adjacent roles are being discharged and the rules are being kept, no member improves his standing — or the group's aim — by defecting from his function. This is recognizably a Nash-style notion, but over *roles* rather than over *beliefs*. It demands no epistemic transparency. It demands only that the functions mesh and that defection be unrewarded given the meshing.

So the slogan "group intention is an equilibrium" was right all along, and so was the deflationary insistence that no further entity is posited — the equilibrium is just a relational property of the members' role-discharges. What was wrong was the choice of variable. The orthodoxy reached for the one equilibrium notion (Aumann's) that quietly re-imports a cognitive precondition the clearest cases lack, because it took its examples from a corner of human life where that precondition happens to be available. Keep the equilibrium; change what it ranges over; and the analysis at once covers ants, firms, and armies — the cases the epistemic reading was forced to either explain away or ignore.

## 7. Objections

*Objection 1: Without common knowledge, what distinguishes a group intention from mere parallel behavior or a market?* A crowd fleeing a fire moves in parallel without a group intention; a market clears without intending anything. The answer is the *role-architecture*. In the crowd there are no defined, interlocking functions and no rules each is "not to break" on expectation of reciprocity; there is only a coincidence of individual aims. A market is closer, but its coordination is achieved through price signals among agents who are precisely *not* playing assigned, mutually-trusting roles in a common task — each pursues his own end and coordination is a byproduct. Role-commitment equilibrium requires assigned functions, governing rules, reciprocal role-trust, and a common aim the meshing realizes. Parallel flight and the market lack these. So the account draws the line in the right place without help from iterated belief.

*Objection 2: Surely some minimal mutual awareness is needed — the private at least believes he is among comrades.* Granted, and harmless. The account requires that each member accept his role *as a role on a team*, which carries a thin, first-order expectation of reciprocity. What it denies is that this thin expectation must be iterated into a lattice of higher-order beliefs, or be common knowledge across the group. A single defeasible default — "others will keep their roles" — is not the orthodoxy's infinite tower, and the ant colony shows that even this can be implemented without representation at all, by reliable dispositions standing in for expectation.

*Objection 3: Doesn't this overgeneralize, so that any functioning system — a thermostat-and-furnace, an immune response — counts as having a group intention?* The account does extend intention-talk down a long continuum, and it should: the marks of collective intention (goal-directedness, robustness, flexible correction toward an aim) genuinely come in degrees and genuinely appear far below the human. Where to plant the word "intention" on that continuum is a terminological decision, not a discovery of a hidden line. What the account refuses is the *gerrymandered* line drawn by the orthodoxy — one that runs not along any difference in coordination but along the presence or absence of a recursion that the central, undisputed cases of group intention do not exhibit anyway.

## 8. Conclusion

The deflationary thesis is correct: a group intention is no extra entity. The equilibrium thesis is correct: a group intention is a self-sustaining configuration of its members. The orthodox build-out is wrong: that configuration is not a lattice of iterated mutual knowledge. Ant colonies have collective intentions with no mind reading any mind; firms intend across an org chart engineered for opacity; armies intend while the private, the captain, and even the general each know almost nothing of the whole. What each member has is role-knowledge and role-trust — the acceptance of himself as a part-with-a-function, bound by rules he expects to be reciprocally honored. The equilibrium that constitutes the group intention is the stable meshing of those role-commitments, a fixed point over functions rather than over beliefs. Lewis and Aumann built a beautiful apparatus for a problem the phenomenon rarely poses; the honest analysis of group intention is structural and normative, not epistemic-recursive.
