The Specification Bottleneck
The reigning meme holds that AI replaces people. It does—but the exception is not the trivial one. Yes, someone must build and maintain the machine. Set that aside. The deeper counterexample is this: an AI does only what it is told, and telling it is itself a high-order act. Consider two men. X is a dullard; Y is a literary genius. Both instruct Claude to write a novel about a man who turns into a bat. X says exactly that, or worse, "write a good novel," and receives schlock—not because the machine failed, but because his instruction failed. The space of novels consistent with such a command is astronomically large, and the subset of good ones is vanishingly small. An instruction is worth precisely the information it carries about which region of that space to enter. X's instruction carries almost none. It names the target without constraining toward it.
Y's instructions are surgical. Each is a constraint that prunes the bad branches, and the capacity to issue them is as much a product of his genius as the novel he would have written by hand in the pre-AI age. So the labor has not vanished; it has migrated—from generation to specification. AI drives the cost of producing text to nearly zero while leaving the cost of judgment untouched. Judgment therefore becomes the whole bottleneck. This is not a footnote to the replacement thesis. It is the thesis's undoing at the only point that matters.
One qualification, which is also a prophecy. As Y's instructions grow fine enough to reliably hit the good region, they approach being the novel itself—at which point Claude has written nothing and Y has written everything, longhand. The live zone is the middle one: the instruction far shorter than its output yet sufficient to compel excellence. What survives there is not the generative faculty but the discriminative one, and these are not the same organ. A superb editor need not write well; a great writer may specify clumsily. AI thus does not preserve literary genius whole. It keeps the half that is taste and discards the half that is mere production—which tells us exactly who inherits the new world. Not the best writers. The best judges of writing.
