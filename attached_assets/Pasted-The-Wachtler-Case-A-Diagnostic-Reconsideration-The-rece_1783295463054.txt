The Wachtler Case: A Diagnostic Reconsideration
The received account of Sol Wachtler's 1992 fall casts it as the product of bipolar disorder aggravated by a reckless prescription-drug regimen—an illness that happened to an otherwise sound man. But the conduct itself resists that reading. Wachtler's year-long campaign against Joy Silverman was not the diffuse, appetite-driven grandiosity of mania, which spends and gambles and propositions strangers before collapsing into a crash within days. It was targeted, sustained, and strategic: surveillance, threatening letters mailed to evade detection, the invention and disciplined deployment of a fictional alias ("David Purdy") to menace Silverman's daughter. The trigger was abandonment, not a mood upswing; the entire apparatus was organized around a single person's rejection and the intolerable experience of being discarded. That is the signature of borderline organization, not a mood disorder—pathological in motivation, perhaps, but methodical and lucid in execution, carried out by a man who continued deciding appellate cases at a high level throughout. Whatever illness he had never once cost him the capacity to function; it explains the wanting, not a loss of control.
The counterfactual settles it. A woman who stalked an ex after a breakup, impersonated others to threaten his family, and triangulated a child into her campaign would be labeled borderline almost reflexively, with a note about attachment pathology. Wachtler received "bipolar" because it is the more dignified, less characterological label—the one that says an illness befell me rather than this is the structure of my personality—and because psychosis, when it occurs at all, can stabilize into enduring traits that operate at baseline, in ordinary lucid time. Invoking those traits as exculpatory is therefore circular: "I did it because of who I am" is a description, not a defense. The more economical reading is of a man competent in a narrow professional lane but arrested beneath it, who had spent decades in a world where his status reliably got him off the hook, met the first loss his position could not fix, and responded not like a sick man but like a powerful one who had never learned the limits of power.
