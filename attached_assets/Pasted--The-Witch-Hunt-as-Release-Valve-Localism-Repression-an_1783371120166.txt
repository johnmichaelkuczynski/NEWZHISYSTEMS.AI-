# The Witch Hunt as Release Valve: Localism, Repression, and the Bodies That Followed

The standard account of Europe's witch panics — that the Catholic–Protestant rivalry drove them — is a red herring that collapses on its own counterexamples. Britain hunted witches as a solidly Protestant society; Scotland, fiercely Calvinist, was among the deadliest per capita in Europe; Salem had no Catholics to fight at all. What actually predicts the death toll is not which Christ a community prayed to but whether anyone above it could stop the killing. Spain and its colonies routed every accusation through strong central courts that distrusted torture-confessions and quashed panics from above, and so they burned almost no one despite being the most Catholic places on earth. The lethal zones were the opposite: patchwork Germany, kirk-session Scotland, colonial New England — places where a local body could work its will without asking permission upstream. Localism was the enabling condition. It decided whether a community's private appetites ever reached the killing stage or were strangled at the source.

And the appetite is the part the confessional story exists to hide. These were repressive societies that had walled off sex and aggression hard — Calvinist self-scrutiny, bodily shame, the whole apparatus — and a witch trial is a socially licensed release valve for exactly what was dammed. Consider what the trials actually consisted of: the stripping and searching of women's bodies for the devil's mark, obsessive interrogation about intercourse with Satan, the prolonged infliction of pain on the helpless by respectable men who got to call it godly duty. That is discharge dressed as piety — sadism and sexual fixation handed a warrant, aimed overwhelmingly at women who were old, poor, and unprotected. It needs no germ theory or metaphysics of meaninglessness to explain it. It needs only repression to build the pressure and localism to remove the brake. Uptight communities generate the charge; weak central authority lets it fire; and the trial is where it comes out. That is a nastier and more human explanation than anything about pacts and sabbaths — and it is the one the theology was always there to obscure.
