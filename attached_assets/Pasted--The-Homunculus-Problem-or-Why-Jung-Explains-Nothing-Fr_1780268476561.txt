# The Homunculus Problem, or Why Jung Explains Nothing

Freud and Jung both confront the same datum: a man is one thing on Monday and the opposite on Tuesday. The chickenshit who obeys his wife and his boss for twenty years suddenly grows a spine, starts his own business, and walks out of the marriage. Both men want to account for this. Only one of them can.

Freud's account is conservative and, for that reason, explanatory. There is one person. A personality is by definition the totality of the drives, defenses, sublimations, and compromises sloshing around in a single architecture. The assertive impulses were always there; they were held down by fear, and by whatever the marriage and the job were paying out—security, an alibi for not risking himself. Then the balance of forces shifted. The fear lost a battle it had been winning. Nothing new is posited. The same man owns the cowering and owns the breakout, and his misery during the cucked years is intelligible precisely because the thwarted impulse was *his*, pressing against a defense that was also his. That is what an explanation looks like: forces within one architecture, one subject responsible throughout.

Jung wrecks this, and he wrecks it with a single bad habit—personification. He does not speak of impulses and defenses; he speaks of the shadow, the anima, the Self, each a quasi-agent you "meet" and "dialogue with." But the moment you turn an impulse into a little self, you have not deepened the explanation, you have destroyed it. This is the homunculus problem, and it is fatal. If the wanting-more is a separate self—the shadow—then the cucked man *proper* is just a cuck, fully constituted as such, and on his own terms he should be perfectly content, because the dissatisfaction lives in somebody else. So who suffers during the twenty years? Either the cuck-self suffers, in which case the wanting was his all along and you never needed a second self; or the shadow-self suffers, in which case you must now explain how one self feels another self's frustration—and you are inventing telepathy between homunculi to patch a hole you tore by splitting the man in the first place. Every repair costs another entity. And each new entity is itself a little person needing its own desires, its own defenses, its own reasons—so you are off on a regress with no floor.

The general point follows immediately. Selves at war is a picture; forces in one self is a mechanism. A force model scales across time and situation effortlessly, because "the repressed" was never a thing—it is a *status* some content has at a given moment, under a given pressure, breached under given conditions. Repression is a verb wearing a noun's clothing, and verbs combine, split, intensify, and lapse exactly as the behavior demands. A cast of selves does none of this. Agents do not decompose and recombine; characters do not merge and divide; and so the personified psyche can only be narrated, never tracked. Worse, the narration always fits, because stories always fit—which is to say it has no content.

This is why Jung is useless. Not wrong in every particular—he is often acute when he is merely restating Freud—but useless in his distinctive apparatus, because that apparatus is built on the one move that voids explanation. He mistook vivid personification for depth. Personification is the disease, not the insight. One man, one architecture, variable force: that explains the wallflower and the monster, the cuck and the tyrant, with no second self required and one fewer entity to apologize for. The instant you populate a single skull with a revolving cavalcade of little people at war, you have purchased color at the price of intelligibility, and you have nothing left to say.
