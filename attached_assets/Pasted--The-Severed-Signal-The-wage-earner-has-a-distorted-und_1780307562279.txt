# The Severed Signal

The wage-earner has a distorted understanding of money, and the distortion is structural rather than personal. The self-employed man who lives by his wits has total money-awareness, and he has no alternative: he knows precisely how much a given act of his generates, and precisely how much money he needs to do a given thing, because nothing stands between his conduct and its consequence. Every act produces a number, every need costs a number, and he sees both. The employee — even one in an arduous, well-paid job — does not. He does not transact with the world; he transacts with the institution, and the institution transacts with the world. He is insulated by exactly one layer, and that layer is precisely where money-awareness lives. The firm knows what each act generates and what each thing costs, because the firm faces reality directly. It is the self-employed party. The employee faces only the firm.

His "earning" is not earning at all but a *draw* against the firm's earning, smoothed into a wage that arrives on a schedule bearing no visible relation to what he produced this week or what the market actually paid for it. The signal that would create money-awareness — *this act produced X, this need costs Y* — is severed at the boundary of the institution. He gets a flat, periodic number instead of a live feed. And you cannot develop an organ you never use. So his distorted mentality is not a character flaw; it is the cognitive shadow of the structure, the way wage-conscription shows up in the head rather than the wallet. Take away the signal and you take away the faculty that the signal would have built.

Two distortions follow. First, money becomes a *constant* rather than a *variable* — something that simply appears, like weather, rather than something caused by specific acts — so the employee loses the felt connection between conduct and consequence that fills the self-employed man's every waking hour. Second, and stranger, he *overrates* money's importance precisely because he cannot generate it at will. It becomes a scarce, external, slightly mysterious substance dispensed by an authority, so he venerates it and fears its loss — which is exactly the leverage the institution holds over him. The man who lives by his wits, by contrast, treats money as cheap in the sense that he can always go make more by doing something, and this paradoxically makes him *less* ruled by it. So the wage-monkey ends up in the worst of both conditions at once: money-obsessed and money-illiterate, anxious about the number, ignorant of its causation. That combination is the unmistakable signature of someone who has never once stood on the side of the table where money is made rather than received.
