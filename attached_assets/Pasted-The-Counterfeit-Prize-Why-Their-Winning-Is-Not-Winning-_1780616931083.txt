The Counterfeit Prize: Why Their "Winning" Is Not Winning at All
The preceding articles examined the work of Falvey on self-knowledge and Edgington on conditionals, vagueness, and the preface paradox, and argued that a single procedure runs through all of it: take an entrenched professional doctrine as a fixed datum, and when it collides with a plainer and more certain fact, sacrifice the fact and erect technical apparatus to make the sacrifice appear principled. We then asked what such authors take themselves to be doing, and rejected the flattering answers in turn. They are not pursuing truth, since the procedure shields a doctrine from what refutes it. They are not being rigorous, since the one load-bearing premise is precisely the thing never examined. They are not skillful, since equating a statement with a number is a crude blunder dressed as a maneuver. What remained was small and exact: they believe they are winning. They are making the moves that the profession rewards, and collecting the rewards — the chair, the citation, the festschrift. In this they resemble the executive who climbs by skullduggery or the general who conquers by force. None of these figures imagines himself accurate or rigorous or even good at the underlying craft. Each imagines himself a winner, and in the ordinary sense each is right.
But there is a disanalogy between their winning and the winning of the executive, the general, the tyrant, and it is the whole of the matter. The power that Stalin holds is referential: it points to something outside the act by which it is conferred. The regime that grants it may be monstrous and Stalin himself may be a mediocrity, yet the tanks move when he orders them to move, the populations are deported, the will reaches into the world and rearranges it. Strip away every flattering belief about the man — that he is wise, that he is just, that he earned his place — and the apparatus still obeys. The power was never the belief; the belief was at most a lubricant. The same holds for the executive who actually ends up controlling the firm and the general who actually razes the country. Their prizes are solid. Each survives full disclosure of how it was obtained, because each consists in something held — resources, command, territory, money — that does not depend on anyone's continued misperception. Expose the executive as a fraud and he still runs the company until he is removed. The prize and the esteem are two separate things, and only the second is destroyed by the truth.
The prize that Falvey, Edgington, and others of their kind have won is of an entirely different nature, and the difference is not one of degree. They command no resources. Their salaries are contingent and ordinary, not fortunes. They move nothing in the world; no instrument of force or production answers to them. What they possess is a position in a structure of professional regard — a quantity of esteem internal to a small caste, which exists only so long as that caste continues to perceive the work as work. This is not a thinner version of Stalin's power. It is a different kind of thing, because it has no referent outside the act of its own conferral. The regard is not a sign that points to some held asset; the regard is the asset. And an asset that consists in being esteemed is annihilated at the instant the esteem is withdrawn. There is no residue — no held ground, no controlled firm, no money in the account — that outlasts the moment of disclosure. The tyrant can be unmasked and keep the tanks. These men, unmasked, are left holding nothing, because there was never anything in their hands but other people's agreement to see something that was not there.
This is why their winning is, on inspection, not winning at all, but a standing wave in the confusion of others — a shape that persists only as long as the medium sustaining it persists, and vanishes without trace when it does not. The executive's fraud is incidental to his prize; their fraud is constitutive of theirs. What they have achieved is to be mistakenly credited, and to have arranged their lives so that the mistaken crediting never gets corrected — by never asking the one question that would correct it, and by building a system that pays its members not to ask it either. So the final accounting is starker than calling them poor philosophers. A poor philosopher has at least entered the contest of trying to say something true and lost. These have not entered it. They have won a contest that runs alongside it, whose prize is made entirely of regard and dissolves the moment regard is examined — and they have mistaken that dissolving thing for the solid prizes that real winners hold. They believe they are winning. In the only sense in which winning would mean anything — possessing something that survives the truth about how you got it — they have won nothing, and never will, because the thing they have spent their lives accumulating is precisely the thing that cannot survive being looked at.
