# America Worked Too Well

The standard complaint about the United States is that the system failed. This is almost exactly backwards. The system did not fail. It succeeded so completely, and so fast, that it ran past the limits of what its beneficiaries knew how to do with the results. The real story of American decline — to the extent there is one — is not a story of breakdown. It is a story of a machine that overdelivered, handing out freedom and money to tens of millions of people on a scale no civilization had ever managed, and then offering no instructions for what to do once you had them.

Consider the scale of the achievement before judging the aftermath. Within a few generations the country took ordinary people — not aristocrats, not a credentialed elite, just whoever showed up — and made them freer and richer than the kings of earlier centuries. A middle-class family in postwar California had more mobility, more privacy, more leisure, more legal protection, and more buying power than almost anyone alive a hundred years before. This was not luck. It was the predictable output of a system built to convert effort, property, and time into compounding prosperity. The engine worked exactly as designed.

The trouble is that the design had no failure mode for excess. Every serious system is hardened against scarcity, war, and collapse. None is hardened against *success* — against the problem of what happens when a free people gets wealthy faster than it builds the character to carry the wealth. And so two things followed, both of them downstream of the system working too well rather than too poorly.

The first is the accidental fortune. The country was so good at generating asset wealth that enormous sums landed on people who did nothing to earn them beyond holding a deed when the curve went vertical. Santa Barbara is the clearest specimen: people of no particular distinction became rich simply by buying a house at the right moment. But you can see the same thing driving through Madison, or Portland, or any city where ordinary timing produced extraordinary balance sheets. Whether those people did anything worthwhile with the money is a separate question, and maybe an unanswerable one. The point is that if they squandered it, that is not the country failing. That is the country succeeding so thoroughly that it minted a class of beneficiaries with no plan for their own good fortune.

The second is the regulatory ratchet. A people unaccustomed to handling risk, but newly empowered to demand that someone else absorb it, will reliably ask the state to smooth over every rough edge of its own freedom. One vice law here, one licensing requirement there, one new credential to gatekeep one more trade — each individually defensible, each sold as a small fix to a small problem. No one ever voted, in a single sitting, for the overbearing apparatus that resulted. It accreted, micro-fix by micro-fix, out of a refusal to tolerate the downsides of the very liberty the system had delivered. The state became heavy not because it failed, but because free and comfortable people kept asking it to protect them from the consequences of being free and comfortable.

So when the apparatus finally turned oppressive — and in places it did — the honest description is not that the engine broke. It is that the engine ran perfectly, threw off more wealth and more freedom than its passengers could metabolize, and was then gradually weighed down by their own demands until it handled badly. A car that runs like a charm is not an engineering failure because someone loads it with sandbags and complains it won't corner.

The generalization is this: there is no backup plan for too much success, and there almost never is, anywhere. We design for hardship and forget to design for triumph. America's deepest problem was never that it failed its people. It was that it gave them more than they had any framework to handle — and then let them blame the gift.
