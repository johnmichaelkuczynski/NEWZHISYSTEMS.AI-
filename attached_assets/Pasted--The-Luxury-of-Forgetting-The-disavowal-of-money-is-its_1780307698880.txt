# The Luxury of Forgetting

The disavowal of money is itself a luxury good. The speech — "it's not really about money, money isn't that important" — is almost never delivered by someone who has none. It is delivered by someone whose supply is so reliable, through salary, tenure, family wealth, or institutional perch, that he has lost sight of it the way a fish loses sight of water. He mistakes the *invisibility* of his money for its *unimportance*, when the invisibility is in fact a precise measure of how total his dependence has become. Every microstructure of his life — where he lives, what he eats, the school his children attend, the leisure he enjoys, the very serenity that permits him to philosophize about money's triviality — is buoyed by a flow he no longer has to think about. The man who can forget money is the man money has most completely provided for. His forgetting is not the refutation of money's importance. It is the proof.

And here is the sharper blade. "Money-driven" is frequently just the slur the implicitly-wealthy aim at the explicitly-earning. The self-employed man's vivid money-awareness is not greed; it is the cognition of someone actually standing in the causal stream, who must know what each act yields because no institution is metabolizing that question on his behalf. He looks money-driven for the same reason a sailor looks weather-obsessed and a passenger does not: one is operating the vessel, the other is along for the ride. The passenger's serenity about the weather is not wisdom. It is insulation. He can afford not to watch the sky because someone else is watching it for him, and that someone else is the very institution whose existence makes his indifference possible.

So the contempt runs exactly backwards from how it is packaged. The salaried man flatters himself that his indifference to money signals higher values, when it actually signals that he is a cell in an organism that does his earning for him. The invidious edge in those speeches — the ill-will underneath the serene tone — is the cell's need to moralize its own dependence into superiority, and to recast the man who lives by his wits as somehow grubby for doing the very thing the speechmaker has merely been excused from. It is the parasite-and-thief inversion again, transposed into the register of money: the one who faces reality directly is called base by the one who has been insulated from it. The thief at least touches the world. The man lecturing him about his money-obsession has arranged never to have to.
