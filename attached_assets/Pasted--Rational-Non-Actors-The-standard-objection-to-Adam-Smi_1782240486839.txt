# Rational Non-Actors

The standard objection to Adam Smith's rational-actor model is that people are too irrational to fit it. The objection is misconceived. The model fails, but not because people are bad calculators. It fails because it locates rationality in the wrong place. Smith's framework grades the *pursuit* of ends while taking the ends themselves as given, exogenous inputs. And it is precisely at the level of ends — of goal-setting — that human beings are least Smithian. People do not arrive at their goals by any optimization they could own. They absorb them. The goals are inherited from the reference group, the species, the troop, and held because the group holds them, not because the agent weighed alternatives and selected the best. At the level where the model assumes a sovereign chooser, there is usually no choosing at all.

Where people *are* reliably Smithian is in execution. Once a goal is fixed — however unreflectively it was acquired — most people pursue it with real shrewdness, economizing on effort, hedging, learning from feedback, and converging on sensible means. This is the half of the picture the model gets right and the half that misleads everyone, because competent execution is easily mistaken for sovereign agency. Watch a person pursue an inherited goal efficiently and you will be tempted to credit him with having rationally chosen it. He did not. He is a good executor of an aim he never authored, which is a different thing entirely from a rational actor in Smith's full sense.

This is why the Kahneman–Tversky program is not the correction it claims to be. TK catalogue "biases" — framing reversals, the conjunction fallacy, anchoring, loss aversion — and conclude that people are irrational. But these are deviations only from a Smithian norm that was never independently justified; they are irrationalities *relative to* expected-utility consistency, smuggled in as though it were the law of thought. Worse, in ordinary life the supposed defects are the opposite of defects. A person who actually satisfied the TK ideal — recomputing the expected utility of every toothpaste brand each morning, deriving his breakfast from scratch, pricing the odds on every trivial node — would be paralyzed. He would burn his entire cognitive budget shaving fractions of a cent off carrots and produce nothing. The heuristics TK call irrational are the controlled inefficiencies that buy reliability and free the mind for anything higher. Being "a bit off" on petty computations is not a flaw bolted onto an otherwise rational agent; it is the price of being a functioning agent at all.

There is one arena where the TK litany genuinely bites: the stock market. The market strips away the two things that normally rescue people from their biases — the social scaffolding and the slow, corrective feedback of repeated consequential choice. It is a one-shot, high-variance, low-feedback environment, which is to say it reproduces laboratory conditions with real money. There, loss aversion makes people hold losers and sell winners; recency and availability make them chase what just rose; anchoring pins them to a purchase price; overconfidence sizes them into the blowup. And the conformist's deepest instinct — take comfort in the consensus, do what the room is doing — becomes the precise mechanism of loss, because by the time the crowd is unanimous the move is already priced. The operating system that earns approval in the seminar gets slaughtered on the tape.

But even this concession should be kept narrow, because the stock market is the exception that reveals the rule. It is the rare environment that rewards autonomous, against-the-room judgment — the willingness to be temporarily and visibly alone — which is exactly the capacity the conformist lacks. His failure there is not a failure of *computation*; it is a failure of authorship, the same absence that defines him everywhere else. He cannot buy the unloved thing or sell the adored one because his identity is organized around not being the odd man out. So even the market, where TK look vindicated, is really illustrating the deeper point: what looks like cognitive error is a downstream symptom of a missing self, not a defect in the arithmetic.

The real problem with Smithian rationality, then, is not that people are irrational actors. It is that they are *rational non-actors*. They execute, often with great efficiency, mandates they never set — species mandates, collective mandates, the inherited program of the group. They are rational in the means and absent at the level of ends. This is why both critiques miss: TK indict the calculation, which is mostly fine and often optimal, while Smith credits an authorship that mostly isn't there. The defect worth naming lives in neither place they are looking. It is upstream of computation and inside the goal itself — not error in the choosing, but the absence of any choosing to begin with. The modal human is not a flawed optimizer of his own ends. He is a competent optimizer of ends that were never his, and the frameworks that quarrel over his rationality have both mistaken a well-run reflex for a deciding subject.
