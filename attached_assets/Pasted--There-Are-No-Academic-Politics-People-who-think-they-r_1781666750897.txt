# There Are No Academic Politics

People who think they're sharp say: "Academic politics are so petty because the stakes are so low." No. That is a stupid statement, and it is wrong twice over. It concedes that there are stakes (just small ones) and that the thing is politics (just petty politics). Both concessions are false.

Politics is exchange between opposing interests. The Democrats get the dam; the Republicans get the deregulation. You give a little to get a little. That requires two things: people who want genuinely different things, and a currency they can trade in. Real politics is logrolling. It is deals.

Academic philosophy has neither. Everyone wants the exact same thing — standing — and there is nothing to swap for it. Your being a rationalist and his being a self-styled "logician" are not interests in tension. They are calling cards. Plumage. So no deal is possible. Not because the people are too small to make deals, but because there is nothing to deal with. Subtract the bargaining and you do not get "petty politics." You get the absence of politics. You get grovel and grovel-plus.

What is left when you remove exchange is a court. Courtiers fighting for the favor of a sovereign who alone confers worth. They trade nothing. They produce nothing. The only currency is the patron's regard. That is why outsiders are not crazy to reach for the word "politics" — they have just grabbed the wrong kind. They picture a legislature. It is a court. And it is lower than insect politics, because the bees are at least allocating actual nectar.

Here is the proof, and I have lived it more times than I can count. Two ranked professors, X and Y, disagree about something foundational. X thinks content externalism is a flat mistake and has decent reasons. Y is an externalist to the marrow. Now an unranked person, Z, walks up to X and says: "I agree with you, and here are additional reasons you're right." In a political world, X banks it. Z is an ally. Free force on your side. You take it.

That is not what happens. In philosophy, Z's agreement is irrelevant — unless Z is, independently, someone who can be brought into the one fold without making anyone feel threatened. The agreement itself does nothing. Worse than nothing.

It happened to me with Barry Stroud. Stroud disagreed with Kaplan — or Fine — about something in a seminar. (Fine and Kaplan hated me.) Afterward, nobody else around, I went up to Stroud. I repeated his position back to him exactly and said: here are additional reasons your view prevails, R1 through Rn. He looked at me nervously. Muttered something evasive. Stared at his feet. Walked away.

That recoil is the whole thing in miniature. Watch the direction of the fear. In a bargaining world, agreement-plus-better-reasons is a costless gift. In a court it is a threat dressed as support, and it threatens two ways. First, accepting help from a nobody contaminates you downward — worth flows from above, and taking a crumb from below inverts the hierarchy you live by. Second, an unranked man who brings *better* reasons than you had says you did not fully own your own position. He saw further. There is no slot in the system for "correct help from a non-person," so Stroud could not metabolize it. He just walked.

And the Kaplan/Fine hatred is the same machine running the other direction. They did not hate me for being wrong. They hated me because I was minting worth-claims — good arguments — without authorization to mint them. An unranked person who is also right is the single most threatening object in a court. He proves the currency was never backed by anything. That is the one secret a court exists to keep.

So state it cleanly. In real politics, being right and being useful convert into standing. In philosophy they do not. Standing is handed down and sideways by people whose regard is the only money there is. A correct argument from outside the fold is not legal tender. It is at best inert. At worst it looks like counterfeit — because it cannot be traced to an authorizing source. That is not politics. There are no politics here. There is a court, and there is the door.
