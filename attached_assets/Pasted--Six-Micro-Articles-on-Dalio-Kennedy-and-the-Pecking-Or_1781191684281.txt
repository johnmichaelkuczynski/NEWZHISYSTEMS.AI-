# Six Micro-Articles on Dalio, Kennedy, and the Pecking-Order Fallacy

## 1. Dalio's Fork: False or Irrelevant

When Ray Dalio writes about "the changing world order," he is writing about one thing: the international pecking order — who ranks where among nation-states, measured by trade share, military reach, financial weight, and reserve-currency status. The entire emotional charge of his project depends on the reader silently translating "America's place in that ranking is slipping" into "life in America is about to get worse." But those are two different variables, and the translation is illegitimate. So Dalio faces a fork, and both tines are fatal. If he *is* equating place in the pecking order with the quality of life of citizens, then what he is saying is straightforwardly, empirically false — the Dutch lost the top slot and lived well, the British shed an empire across the twentieth century while their citizens' lives materially improved, and there is no general link between a state's clout abroad and the texture of life inside it.

If, on the other hand, he is *not* making that equation — if he means only that the ranking of states is shifting — then he is correct and it does not matter, except to the narrow set of people interested in macroeconomics purely as macroeconomics, with no intention of mapping it onto anything that would make it consequential. There is no third option that rescues the gravity of the book. The dread is borrowed entirely from the welfare reading; the defensible content lives entirely in the ledger reading; and Dalio collects the emotional payload of the first while only ever committing to the second.

## 2. The Grab Bag, and the One Real (Borrowed) Idea

Dalio's apparatus is a grab bag of "determinants" of pecking-order rank — education, innovation, competitiveness, trade share, military strength, financial-center status, reserve currency, debt, wealth gaps, internal conflict — bundled into a composite index that traces a tidy rise and fall. But several of these are not determinants of anything; they are the outcome wearing a disguise. "Competitiveness" means *a country produces what the world wants at prices it will pay* — i.e., it is economically successful. So "competitiveness drives a nation's rise" reduces to "success drives success." Strip out the circular items and the downstream symptoms (trade share, reserve status, and the rest are *consequences* of a productive economy, not causes of it) and the list explains nothing, because the variables are partly definitional of the thing they purport to predict. Press him, and the grand theory collapses to a single intelligible claim: a nation with a chip on its shoulder grows; a nation that feels it has arrived reaches for the second donut, eventually topples out of its fat little high chair, and somebody hungrier takes its seat at the buffet. That is a real thesis — and it is Ibn Khaldun's, six hundred and fifty years old, re-skinned in quant clothing without improvement.

Two things sink even this salvaged version. First, it applies, at most, to roughly one-tenth of one percent of nation-states — the USA, the UK, perhaps Russia — the tiny handful grand enough to be called would-be hegemons. You cannot derive a law with a 250-year period and a fixed stage-sequence from an n of three, drawn from non-comparable epochs, with the stages defined *after* you already know how each case ended. That is curve-fitting to hand-picked anecdotes, then selling the fit as prophecy. Second, the thesis says nothing — zero — about quality of life *within* those mega-states; if anything the correlation runs negative, since Stalin's Russia and Mao's China were maximal force-projectors precisely while starving and murdering their own people. And it says nothing, *ipso facto*, about the quality of life in the nations at the bottom of the pecking order. Iceland is close to dead last in Dalio-punch and close to first in how good it is to live there. A theory of "why nations succeed and fail" that structurally cannot see Iceland is a theory of something other than success.

## 3. Kennedy: A Better-Defined Thesis With the Same Buried Equation

Paul Kennedy has a sharper claim than Dalio's: great powers fall when military spending crosses some line in the sand — before which, loosely, each dollar of force buys more than a dollar of advantage, and after which the marginal dollars go negative, the army becoming a millstone that drags the productive base down faster than rivals are growing. This is at least falsifiable, which already puts it a class above the eighteen-variable blob. But Kennedy inherits the same fatal ambiguity. Either he is tacitly equating place in the pecking order with the quality of life of citizens — in which case his "decline" carries the dread he wants but rests on the false equation that the Dutch and British cases destroy — or he is not, in which case "the empire declined" means only that a particular government can now afford fewer carriers relative to a rival, which is a true, narrow, emotionally inert ledger fact that matters to strategists and to no one else. He is more honest than Dalio and stays mostly at the state level, but he still cashes a dread-check his actual claim cannot cover.

## 4. Kennedy: Is the Thesis Even True?

Set the conflation aside and ask whether the mechanism — too many guns crash the empire — actually holds in the marquee cases. It does not, cleanly, in either. The Soviet Union did not fall because military spending crossed a profitable threshold; it fell because it had *no economy*. Yes, it spent heavily on the military — but that was a symptom of central planning having produced nothing else worth counting, not a cause eating a healthy base. The military was the one sector they could force to function. Technically the USSR was not overspending on defense; it was under-producing everything else, and the collapse came from productive *absence*. Kennedy's causal arrow runs backwards here.

Britain is the cleaner refutation. Was the empire really sustained by the military — were Nigeria and Ghana brimming with British regiments? They were not; the whole logic of indirect rule was that the empire ran *cheap*, on prestige, collaboration, and a thin administrative veneer, not on garrisons in every village. What Britain lost was not affordable troops but the *legitimating story*. Britain fought a decade-long war against Germany whose explicit purpose was to prove the illegitimacy of racial hierarchy — and you cannot spend ten years fighting Hitler on that ground and then turn around and justify your own colonies by appeal to a white man's civilizing mission. (This is no verdict on whether British colonialism was good or bad; the point is structural.) Once that element of racial self-belief evaporated, Britain lost both the *will* to project force and the ability to project it by any *non*-military means — there was no longer a message, no longer an assumed right to rule. The decisive variable was legitimacy, not the military line-item. So: could excessive military spending crash an empire? Sure, in principle. Is that categorically what happens? No. Empires fall when the underlying thing gives way — productive capacity, or the belief that sustains the will — and military overspend is usually a *reading* on that deeper gauge, not the gauge itself.

## 5. The Falseness of Hobbesianism in the International Arena

The countries where life is very, very good — Iceland, Switzerland, the Gulf city-states — are precisely the ones at the bottom of the pecking order. Nigeria could crush Iceland militarily a thousand times over; quality of life is far higher in Iceland. The Hobbesian premise shared by Dalio and Kennedy — *if you lack power, you get crushed in the international arena* — is simply, empirically false, and it is worth being exact about *why* it is false, because the reason is a single clean fact about wealth. In the old world, wealth was a stock you seized: quantity of land, bullion, tribute, slaves. There, force was the master tool, because force was how you acquired a fixed pie, and a state good at force genuinely enriched its people. That world is gone. Wealth today is not a stock to be commandeered; it is the ability to dip your cup into the flowing streams of international industrial productivity — to wet your fingertips in the current without falling in.

That single shift falsifies the whole Hobbesian picture of international relations, and it falsifies it *mechanically*, not just statistically. If Nigeria nuked Iceland it would get nothing in return, because commandeering land no longer equals acquiring wealth — and has not for quite some time. You can occupy a territory, but you cannot seize the thing that made it rich: the educated population, the institutions, the trust networks, the tacit know-how, all of which evaporate or refuse to function under a boot. Conquest now destroys the asset it targets. Which is why force-projection has gone from being the engine of acquisition to being a pure cost center — a thing you *spend* prosperity on, not a thing that *creates* it — and why the small, rich, force-less neutrals are not surviving despite their weakness but, in part, prospering *because* they redirected into production everything the garrison states burned on spears.

## 6. The Near-Total Falseness of Hobbesianism in Domestic Affairs

You do not need a Stalin to keep a small society from devouring itself. Tribes run near-zero crime not because a chief stands poised to smash defectors but because the conditions that would make predation pay do not exist: mutual dependency on the hunt, the butchering, the childcare, the defense, plus shared culture and a high degree of biological kinship, install the restraint *inside* people. The lone predator cannot exist when exit from the group is death; betrayal becomes self-destruction, and the Hobbesian calculator — *I'll strike the moment the grip slackens* — never even boots up. And you do not need a Stalin for large-scale society either. Scale requires formal law and some coercion, yes — but the coercion is the thin backstop, not the operating system. The overwhelming majority of order is produced laterally: mutual policing, the supervisor's nudge, reputation, the white-knight reflex, the human eagerness to be the one who stops the mugger and to gain standing by visibly condemning the predator. It is not *the law versus all of society*; it is *society using the law against a few strays*. We did not need an iron hand to make society recoil from a killing and demand consequences — that reflex fires from below, and the courtroom is its formalized tail end, not its source.

And when you *do* find a situation where a Stalin seems genuinely required to keep people from each other's throats, look closely: it is almost always because a Stalin forced those people together in the first place. The "war of all against all" the strongman claims to be suppressing is not natural; it is a *war of group against group*, manufactured by jamming non-consenting peoples into one cage. The proof is what happens when the hand releases. The Soviet empire fell and its captive nations did not dissolve into a Hobbesian melee — they re-sorted into their actual cohesive units and, overwhelmingly, left one another alone. Where blood was shed (Karabakh, Chechnya, Georgia) it was precisely where borders had been drawn across a people or distinct groups had been forced to cohabit — friction generated by the *prior coercion*, not by its removal. The skirmishing is the crossfire thrown off as groups try to mutually self-isolate. Which inverts Hobbes entirely: the Leviathan, in its maximal form, does not cure the war of all against all. It is a parasite that takes credit for a peace it did not create — and, often enough, the very thing that started the fight.
