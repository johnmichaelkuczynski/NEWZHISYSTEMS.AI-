The Poser-Trap: What the Great Books Programs Were Actually For
The standard story is that the Great Books programs—St. John's, Bard, the Hutchins apparatus at Chicago—began with a noble democratic concept and let careerist curators neuter it. That story is too kind. The neutering was the product, not the corruption of it. The concept advertised was dialogue: sit with Hobbes directly, test him, draw a blueprint from his theory, and find out in public whether it holds. But application means exposure, and exposure is the one thing the clientele came specifically to avoid. Draw a Hobbesian blueprint, build the dam, and reality grades your paper where everyone can watch you roll snake eyes. The people who gravitated to these rooms didn't want that risk; they wanted a sealed world of sacred text where the only grading is done by fellow initiates, using criteria that conveniently can never be wrong. They didn't fear the hierarchy. They fled toward it—because a hierarchy of appreciation lets you rise without ever being right about anything testable, mobility purchased with a secret handshake instead of a verifiable result.
This explains the two things the programs reliably produce: a clean bimodal exit and a steady supply of "guardians of the sacred." The split is the tell. The few who saw that the temple was a status-sink left completely, burned the boats, because once the trick is visible there's nothing partial to feel. What stayed wasn't the merely comfortable but the ones who actively wanted the guardianship—who came for the priesthood, with its maximal reverence-signaling, zero exposure, and a permanent license to look down on the uninitiated. And it is specifically a midwit habitat. A genuine first-rater doesn't need the sealed room; he can afford to be checked, because testing is where his edge shows. A true dullard can't run the appreciation game at all. It is the middle band—fluent enough to perform seriousness, not strong enough to survive a world that grades on snake eyes—that the guardian role is engineered to catch. So these institutions are not machines that fail to produce thinkers. They are sorting mechanisms that work perfectly, shaped to trap exactly the temperament that wants the feeling of thought with none of its exposure.
